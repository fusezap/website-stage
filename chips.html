The program

Bring-up is a string of small wins.

One test passes, then the next. A model says its first word, and it’s the right one. Then someone else’s code calls your chip like any other endpoint, and nothing in that code had to change.

That last moment is the one we work towards. Every step on the way ends in something you can open and verify for yourself.

We aim for weeks to a first model, not quarters.

simulator first.

Before you write to us

Questions chip teams ask.

More in the FAQ →

We only have a simulator. Is that enough?

Yes, to start. A backend needs a way to compile a kernel and a way to launch it, so a functional simulator with that interface works before silicon exists. On a simulator we prove the answers are right; speed tuning waits for real hardware. That’s how we brought up Inferentia2.

Can we work under NDA?

Yes. We work under NDA, on your hardware or in your environment if you prefer. Nothing about your chip goes into work we do for anyone else, and your chip goes on the public matrix only when you say so.

We have our own compiler. Do you replace it?

No. We use your low-level toolchain and driver to compile and launch kernels, and we call any tuned kernels you already have, such as matrix multiply or attention. We replace the layers above them: fusion, scheduling, kernel generation and serving. Today our renderer emits a C dialect or a Python tile language; LLVM- or MLIR-level entry points are possible but not built yet.

Who owns the backend?

You do. The code we write for your chip is yours, under Apache-2.0, handed over in full with its tests, so your team can run it without us.

What does it cost?

The assessment is free. After that, it’s a 90-day pilot paid in stages tied to results we agree up front. The details are in the FAQ.
