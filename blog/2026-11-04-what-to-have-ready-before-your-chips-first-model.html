What to have ready
before your chip’s first model.

The week before bring-up, the list in your head is long. The one we ask for is short. A toolchain that can do three things, one engineer who knows it, and the models your customers ask for.

4 November 2026 · The FuseZap team

It’s the week before bring-up. Monday is already blocked out in the calendar. The silicon is on its way, or the simulator build is finally stable, and you’re making a list in your head.

The list keeps growing. A compiler that isn’t finished. A runtime with sharp edges. A serving stack nobody has written. Models, so many models. A team that’s already tired.

This post is about a shorter list. It’s what we ask a chip team to have ready, and why each thing is on it. We learned most of it on a chip we didn’t design.

The list is shorter than the one in your head

Here is the whole of it. A kernel toolchain that can compile a kernel, launch it and manage device memory, or a functional simulator with the same interface. One engineer who knows that toolchain. The models your customers ask for. And access to the hardware or the simulator, in your own environment if you prefer.

That’s it.

Notice what isn’t there. You don’t need a serving stack. You don’t need model code. You don’t need an OpenAI-compatible API, or a way to check that a model’s answers are right. We bring the engine, the server and the checks.

You also don’t need to give anything up. You keep the toolchain and driver you already ship. We use them to compile and launch kernels, and we call the tuned kernels you already have, such as matrix multiply or attention. What we write for your chip is one backend, in four parts. It’s Apache-2.0, and it’s yours, handed over with its tests.

So if the list in your head is keeping you up, put most of it down. Not all of it is yours to carry.

A toolchain that can do three things

A backend needs very little from your chip to start. It needs three things from your toolchain.

Compile. Turn generated kernel source into something a host process can load. Today our renderer writes a C dialect or a Python tile language.

Launch and wait. Start a kernel with buffer addresses and a few integer arguments, wait for it to finish, and time it.

Memory. Allocate and free device buffers, and copy between host and device.

For data types, float32 and int32 are enough to begin. float16 or bfloat16 come later, for speed.

If the silicon isn’t back yet, that’s fine. A functional simulator with the same interface is enough. That’s how we started on AWS Inferentia2: on AWS’s simulator, before the real chip. On the simulator we proved the answers. Speed waited for hardware.

Your toolchain doesn’t have to be finished. It has to do those three things, even slowly. Slow is where everyone starts. On Inferentia2, our own first attempt compiled and loaded each kernel again on every call, about 1.3 seconds a call. We fixed that the same day, by compiling once and keeping the kernel loaded.

We’ll be honest about where our side isn’t ready. Our generated kernels assume flat device memory today. Software-managed scratchpads with explicit DMA are being built for Inferentia2 now. Integer division and modulo are used for indexing, and emulating them on a chip without them isn’t built yet. LLVM- or MLIR-level entry points are possible, but not built. The full list is our hardware capability contract.

And if your chip doesn’t fit that list cleanly, you’re in good company. Inferentia2 didn’t either. Shapes are fixed when a kernel is compiled. Its kernel language works on whole tiles, not threads. Its compiler has to run in a separate process. None of it stopped the bring-up. It just became a list we wrote down.

One engineer, not a team

We ask for one engineer. Not a team, and not your best people pulled off the next tape-out.

One person who knows the toolchain. Someone who can say why a kernel won’t compile, which flag the runtime wants, or where device memory goes when it disappears.

That’s where bring-up time really goes. Every exception on our Inferentia2 list was a question about the toolchain, not about the model. Fixed shapes. Tiles, not threads. A compiler in its own process. Each one is the kind of question that someone who knows the toolchain can answer quickly, and someone new to it can’t.

On your chip, your engineer knows those answers already. We bring everything above the toolchain. They bring what only someone inside your team can know.

It also means the backend doesn’t leave with us. The person who sat next to it from week 0 is the person who’ll run it after the handover.

The models your customers ask for

You already know the question. Before anyone asks about your architecture, they ask: “Will it run Llama?”

So bring the names. Not a cloud’s whole catalogue. The few models your customers have actually asked about, in the order they asked.

Those names tell us which model family comes first. To your chip, a thousand fine-tunes of one architecture are one architecture. We wrote about that in The mountain is smaller than it looks.

Then we start small, inside that family. On Inferentia2, the first model was Qwen2.5-0.5B. On 27 October all 1,441 of its tokens matched the full-precision reference. It was slow. It was right. Two days later, on 29 October, Qwen2.5-7B from the same family ran across both cores of one chip, behind the same OpenAI-compatible API.

Right first. Fast after. The model your customer asked for comes in that order too.

What week 0 hands back

The first week isn’t a pitch. It’s a test run. We run our operation tests on your toolchain or your simulator, on your hardware or in your environment if you prefer. Under NDA, if you need it.

What comes back is an op-coverage report. What runs today, and what it would take to get the rest. After it, an honest estimate for your first model. The assessment is free.

Expect a few names on it you don’t like. On Inferentia2, our first full run on the chip, on 26 October, passed 1,171 of 1,174 operation tests. The 3 that failed were float32 precision in exp2 and tanh. A wider check then found a log2 bug that had passed the suite. That week is its own post: Three tests out of 1,174.

It’s a strange relief, a list of failures with names on it. You stop wondering how far away the first model is. You can see the road.

A checklist for your team

Here’s the list we’d hand your team the week before. Print it, or paste it into the bring-up ticket.

  1. 01CompileYour toolchain turns generated kernel source into something a host process can load, at runtime or ahead of time.
  2. 02Launch and waitIt launches a kernel with buffer addresses and integer arguments, waits for it to finish, and times it.
  3. 03Device memoryIt allocates and frees device buffers, and copies between host and device.
  4. 04float32 and int32Enough to begin. float16 or bfloat16 later, for speed.
  5. 05No silicon yet?A functional simulator with the same interface is enough to start. Prove the answers there.
  6. 06One engineerSomeone who knows that toolchain, and can answer its questions.
  7. 07The model namesThe ones your customers ask for, in the order they asked.
  8. 08AccessTo the hardware or the simulator, in your environment if you prefer. An NDA, if you need one.
  9. 09If you have themYour tuned matrix-multiply and attention kernels. They’re optional, and we’ll call them instead of generating our own.

And one question to take into Monday’s meeting. If someone ran our operation tests on your toolchain this week, which ones would you expect to fail, and why?

If you’d rather find out than guess, that’s week 0. The details are on the Bring-Up Program page.
