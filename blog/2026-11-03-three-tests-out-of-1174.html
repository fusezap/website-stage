Three tests
out of 1,174.

Our first week on AWS Inferentia2, a chip we didn’t design. The simulator first. One new backend. A 22-minute run with three failures. Then a first model that was slow, and right.

3 November 2026 · The FuseZap team

The first full run on the real chip took 22 minutes. When it finished, 1,171 of 1,174 operation tests had passed. Three hadn’t.

If you’ve brought up silicon, you know how that moment really reads. Not 99.7%. Three. Three test names you’ll carry around until they’re gone.

This is our first week on AWS Inferentia2. It’s a chip we didn’t design, with a toolchain that isn’t ours, and it was our first accelerator that isn’t a GPU. We’re telling it in detail because it’s the closest we’ve come to sitting in your seat.

Before the chip, a simulator

We didn’t start on the chip. We started on AWS’s simulator.

A FuseZap backend needs two things from a chip: a way to compile a kernel, and a way to launch it. A functional simulator with the same interface is enough to begin. So that’s where the first tests ran, and where they first passed. All of them.

On the simulator we proved the answers. Speed could wait for real hardware.

Nobody outside the team sees that kind of win. It still counts. When a test later fails on the chip, you already know the test is sound. You look at the chip, not at your own work.

One backend, and a chip that didn’t quite fit

We wrote one new Core backend for Inferentia2. Two parts of it did the real work.

A renderer turns our kernels into NKI, AWS’s kernel language. A device compiles each kernel once with AWS’s compiler, caches it on disk, loads it once into a long-lived runtime process, and launches it on buffers that stay on the chip.

Everything above the backend stayed as it was: the server, the model code, the API.

The chip didn’t fit our interface cleanly, and we didn’t expect it to. Shapes are fixed when a kernel is compiled. The kernel language works on whole tiles, not threads. The compiler has to run in a separate process. We write each of these exceptions down and publish them, with how we handled them.

Your chip will have its own list. We’d rather see it early than meet it late.

26 October: 22 minutes

Our first attempt on the real chip, earlier that day, compiled and loaded each kernel again on every call. About 1.3 seconds a call, whatever the size of the work.

Compiling once and keeping the kernel loaded brought a launch down to about 160–170 microseconds. Then the detail that tells you where to look next: of a 163-microsecond launch, about 8 microseconds were spent on the chip. The chip was the fast part. The rest was on the host side.

Then the full run. 22 minutes. 1,171 of 1,174 operation tests passed on the chip, and our matrix-multiply and attention kernels passed all 81 of their tests.

The three that failed were float32 exp2 (two tests) and tanh (one). The chip computes these with built-in approximations that are a little less precise than our tests allow. Not a crash. Not a wrong kernel. A tolerance, missed by a little.

Then a wider check found something worse. We ran the math functions on their own, 262,144 inputs per case, against a float64 reference. log2 gave wrong answers for some inputs. It had passed the operation tests.

That’s the one that stays with you. Not the test that failed. The bug that passed.

By the next day, exp, exp2 and log2 matched the simulator bit for bit, and the operation suite ran on the chip with no failures.

27 October: slow, and right

We started with the small model we always start with, Qwen2.5-0.5B. We checked its output on our 24 reference prompts against a float32 reference.

24 of 24 prompts matched exactly. So did every one of the 1,441 tokens.

It was slow. About 161 milliseconds a token, because each token took 245 separate kernel launches at about 0.66 ms each. The launches were most of the time.

We knew where the speed was. We still checked the answers first. A fast model that’s wrong tells you nothing about your chip. A slow one that’s right tells you every layer underneath it is telling the truth. If you’ve waited for a first token from new silicon, you know which of those you want to see first.

29 October and 1 November: a real model, then the first token

On 29 October, Qwen2.5-7B ran split across both cores of one Inferentia2, served behind FuseZap’s OpenAI-compatible API. That’s how a customer would use it. We checked it the way we check any release, with FuseZap Validate. Its accuracy and speed runs both ended in PROMOTE, with no failed requests.

Per token, it was a little faster than AWS’s own library on the same kind of machine. To the first token, AWS was quicker. We didn’t like that.

So on 1 November we rewrote the kernels that process the prompt. The first token went from 90.6 to 55.4 milliseconds, and the answers stayed the same, bit for bit. We’re still a few milliseconds behind. There was a faster option, and we turned it down. That’s its own post.

Every number in this post, with the machine, the SDK versions and the full test tables, is in the Inferentia2 notes.

What we’d bring to your bring-up

None of this is new to a chip team. Seeing it from the other side of the table made a few things plain.

Simulator first. If you have a functional simulator, a backend can start there, before the silicon or before your next slot on the bench. Prove the answers on it. Tune the speed on hardware.

One test at a time. Operations before models. A small model before a big one. Count failures by name, not by percentage. Three names are a plan. 0.3% is a mood.

Check wider than your tests. Our log2 bug passed the suite. A plain sweep against a float64 reference caught it.

Right before fast. A first model that’s slow and right is a better week than one that’s fast and nearly right. The speed work goes faster, too, when you trust every layer under it.

Then measure against the best software for your chip. For Inferentia2 that was AWS’s own library. It’s the comparison your customers will make, so make it first.

Here’s a question to take to your next team meeting. If one of your math functions were wrong for some inputs, which of your tests would catch it?

If you’d like to find out on your chip, the first step is a week of our operation tests on your toolchain or your simulator, and an honest map of what runs.
