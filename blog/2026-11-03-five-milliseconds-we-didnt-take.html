Five milliseconds
we didn’t take.

On Inferentia2 a faster setting would have put our first token level with AWS’s own library. It wasn’t bit for bit, so we shipped the exact one. Right first, then fast, with the numbers, and the other time this month speed and people pulled in different directions.

3 November 2026 · The FuseZap team

In one run on 30 October, our first token on Inferentia2 came in at about 50 milliseconds. AWS’s own library, on the same kind of machine, takes 50.6. Level, near enough. The headline was right there.

We didn’t ship it.

The setting behind that number made the chip’s arithmetic slightly different from the arithmetic we had checked. So we shipped the exact one, at 55.4 milliseconds, and we live with being about 5 ms behind. It bothers us. This is why we’d make the same call again, and what it has to do with your chip.

Where the first token started

On 29 October, Qwen2.5-7B ran split across both cores of one Inferentia2, behind our OpenAI-compatible API. Per token, it was a little faster than AWS’s library. To the first token, it wasn’t: 90.6 milliseconds against 50.6.

The first token is the part a person feels. It’s the pause between pressing enter and seeing the answer begin. Everything after it streams. That pause is what people mean when they say a model feels slow.

Most of our 90 ms was in the kernels that read the prompt. A 128-token chunk of prompt took about 82 ms of device time. And one detail stood out: the exact exponential we use, inside attention and the MLP, costs about 45 instructions per element on the chip’s vector engine.

Two ways to compute one exponential

We rewrote the prompt kernels, and we built them with two settings for that exponential.

Exact keeps the exponential we had already checked. Same instructions, same order, same bits.

Fast moves it to another engine on the chip, which computes it in fewer instructions and is a little less exact: off by about one part in 100,000. That’s inside the tolerance we allow for bf16. It was the setting that measured about 50 ms.

The exponential wasn’t the only cost. The old kernels moved data the slow way. Attention alone made about 1,100 separate memory transfers per layer, fetching queries, keys and outputs a token at a time. The new attention kernel makes about 34. And each block of weights, once loaded, now feeds every 128-row block of the prompt instead of being fetched again for each one.

The exact setting, served on 1 November, brought the first token from 90.6 to 55.4 milliseconds.

Then we checked it the hard way. We compared the model’s output for the last prompt token, bit for bit, at prompts of 200, 400 and 512 tokens. We ran each one in 128-token rounds, in one 512-token round, and through the old kernels. Every comparison was identical.

Why we shipped the slower one

“Inside the tolerance” is an easy sentence to say and a hard one to defend.

Before we publish a speed number, the model is checked against a full-precision reference. When we say a change doesn’t change the answers, we check it bit for bit. The exact kernels let us keep saying that. The fast ones would have turned every check we’d already done into a question: was it right then, and is it still right now?

We’ve been on the other side of that question on this same chip. On 26 October, three of our tests failed because the chip’s built-in exp2 and tanh were a little less precise than our tests allow. We fixed them with exact versions. We weren’t going to reopen that for five milliseconds.

The answers could have differed only slightly. But slightly is a word we’d have to explain to every customer who compared outputs, forever. Bit for bit needs no explaining.

So we’re about 5 ms behind AWS’s library to the first token. We say so on the status page, next to the number. Closing it without moving the bits is the top of our list.

The other trade: four people and one big round

The new kernels came with a second size of round. A long prompt could now run in one 512-token round instead of four 128-token ones. A 512-token prompt got its first token in about 199 ms instead of 227.

Our scheduler picks the cheapest way to cover the prompts that are waiting. When four short prompts arrived together, one 512-row round cost less compute in total than four small ones. So it bundled them.

We saw it in Validate’s four-user runs: short prompts, four at a time.

The machine did less work. Every one of those four people waited longer. Each of them now waited for the whole big round to finish. At four users, the typical first token went from 145 to 204 milliseconds.

Cheaper for the chip. Worse for everyone waiting on it.

We changed the rule. Now the oldest waiting prompt alone decides the size of the round. A burst of short prompts runs in short rounds. A long prompt still gets its big round, and short ones ride along in its spare rows.

Served again on 1 November, the typical first token at four users came back to 142 ms. A 512-token prompt still got its first token in about 199 ms instead of 227. The answers were the same, bit for bit, and Validate said PROMOTE.

Total compute is the machine’s number. The first token is a person’s.

What your customers will judge first

Your customers will try the answers before they time them. If the answers are wrong, or merely different from what they got last week, the speed never comes up.

When they do time them, they won’t time your chip’s total throughput. They’ll time the pause before the first word, with a few other people on the same machine.

Your chip probably has faster, less exact paths too: a built-in function, a lower-precision accumulate, a fused step that rounds once instead of twice. Use them. Just know, bit for bit, what each one changes, and choose on purpose. And when you tune a scheduler, measure the person who waits, not only the work that gets done.

Here’s a question to take to your team. Which of your fast paths have you compared, bit for bit, against the slow one?
