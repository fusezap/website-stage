The mountain is
smaller than it looks.

A cloud’s models page can feel like a verdict on your year. It’s counting something else. Underneath sit a handful of model families. We know, because we climbed the first one on a chip we didn’t design.

2 November 2026 · The FuseZap team

The board on your bench has just said its first right word. Then someone sends you a link to a cloud’s models page, and you start scrolling. Chat models. Code models. Image, video, speech. Hundreds of them. Sometimes more than a thousand.

Somewhere around the second screen, the scroll stops feeling like a list. It starts to feel like a verdict on your year.

You already know what your first customer will ask. Not about the memory system, or the architecture you spent years on. They’ll ask: “Will it run Llama?”

That page is a mountain. It just isn’t the one your chip has to climb.

Look closer at the mountain

Read those lists slowly and a pattern shows up. Most entries are a few model shapes wearing different weights. A base model and its chat version. The same model at four sizes. A dozen fine-tunes of one popular release. An image model with a long tail of styles trained on top.

And nearly all of it is served on one kind of hardware: NVIDIA GPUs.

So the number at the top of the page counts a catalogue. That’s a fair measure of what a cloud offers the people who use it. It says very little about what your chip has to support.

To your chip, a thousand fine-tunes of one architecture are one architecture.

What your chip actually climbs

Under the catalogue sit a handful of model families. Roughly:

  • Dense decoder language models: the chat and code models most people mean when they say “an LLM”.
  • Mixture-of-experts language models, where each token goes through a few of many expert layers.
  • Diffusion transformers for images, such as FLUX.
  • Video models.
  • Vision-language models, which read an image and answer in text.
  • Speech models, for audio in and out.

Each family is built from basic operations your chip has to get right. In FuseZap, a model breaks down into 28 of them. Each family also needs a few tuned kernels for the operations that decide speed, like matrix multiply and attention.

The hard parts live at the family level. A mixture-of-experts model has to send each token to the right experts. A diffusion model runs the same network again and again over a picture. Those ask for operations and kernels a dense language model never needed. That’s why each new family is real work.

But once a family runs correctly on your chip, a new model built the same way should be mostly new weights: the same operations in a new combination, with little or no new kernel work. Differences inside a family still need checking: a new tokenizer, a change to attention, another quantisation, a longer context.

Not a thousand problems. Six or so. Each one is real work. None of them is a mountain on its own.

We climbed the first one on a chip we didn’t design

We don’t say this from a distance. We brought up AWS Inferentia2, our first chip that isn’t a GPU. We started where you might be starting now: before the real chip, on AWS’s simulator.

We wrote one new backend. A renderer that turns our kernels into AWS’s NKI kernel language, and a device that compiles each kernel once and keeps it loaded on the chip. Nothing about Qwen2.5 had to be rewritten for it.

26 October. The first full run on the real chip took 22 minutes. 1,171 of 1,174 operation tests passed. The 3 that failed were float32 precision in exp2 and tanh, and a wider check found a bug in log2. Earlier that same day, our first attempt had compiled and loaded each kernel again on every call: about 1.3 seconds a launch. Compiling once and keeping it loaded brought that down to about 160–170 microseconds. You know that kind of day. Almost everything works, and the few things that don’t keep you up.

27 October. The first model. Qwen2.5-0.5B answered our 24 reference prompts on the real chip, and all 1,441 tokens matched the full-precision reference. It was slow, about 161 milliseconds a token. It was right, and on new silicon that’s the one you count first.

29 October. A 7B model from the same family, Qwen2.5-7B, split across both cores of one chip, behind the same OpenAI-compatible API. FuseZap Validate’s verdict was PROMOTE. Per token, it was a little faster than AWS’s own library. To the first token, AWS was quicker.

1 November. We rewrote the kernels that process the prompt. The first token went from 90.6 to 55.4 milliseconds, with the same answers, bit for bit.

Through all of it, the server, the model code and the API above the backend didn’t change. That’s what climbing one family feels like from the inside. Not a thousand models. One family, made right on one more chip.

Where we were on 2 November

Today, 6 November: dense language models and one image model, FLUX.2 [klein], run on FuseZap. Support varies by chip; the updates below and the matrix have the details.

One family. Dense language models ran on FuseZap, and the ones we’d tested end to end were Qwen2.5 and Llama 3.3.

They ran on NVIDIA GPUs, on one H100 or with a 70B model split across two H200s. Qwen2.5 also ran on Apple silicon, on CPU, which is our yardstick for getting answers right, and on Inferentia2. The details, chip by chip, are on the matrix.

On Inferentia2 we were still about 5 ms behind AWS’s library to the first token. A faster setting existed. It changed the answers slightly, so we shipped the exact one. Those 5 ms bother us. We’d still make the same choice.

Here’s what we didn’t run yet. No mixture-of-experts models, and no models with sliding-window attention. No image, video or audio models. Text models only, at that point. It’s all on the status page, with the target next to what we’ve measured.

Next, we were exploring diffusion models for images, such as FLUX, as our first family beyond language. Exploring meant just that: we were looking hard at the work, and not promising a date.

Update, 4 November. We stopped exploring and started building. FLUX.2 [klein], an image model, now makes 1024×1024 pictures on FuseZap on an Apple GPU, behind an OpenAI-style images API. Its images are closer to the full-precision ones than diffusers’ (33.9 dB against 25.5), but it’s about 12 times slower for now, and making it fast is the next job. It’s on the status page, slow number and all.

Update, 5 November. The same image model now also runs on NVIDIA, on one H100 (3.2 seconds an image, about 7 times slower than diffusers for now), an H200 and an RTX 4090, and on two AWS chips, Inferentia2 and Trainium1. On the AWS chips its images match the full-precision ones closely (35.0 and 35.3 dB at 1024×1024, against a bar of 17), but each step takes about 28 to 30 seconds. Right first, again. The details are on the matrix.

We’re also testing our own bet. We believe a second model on a new chip costs far less than the first. We’re measuring that on Inferentia2 now, with a structurally different model, and we’ll publish the number whichever way it comes out.

For your next planning meeting

If you lead a chip team, here’s what we’d bring into the room.

Count families, not models. The useful question isn’t how many models your chip runs. It’s which families, and how well.

Ask which families your customers need. A chip sold for chat and code needs different families first from a chip sold for images. Start with the ones that pay. The rest can wait their turn.

Get each one right before you make it fast. Right means the same answers as a full-precision reference, token for token, not a demo that looks plausible. That’s the job of FuseZap Validate.

Then keep it right. A new driver, a compiler change or a new model can break a family that worked last month. Validate re-checks every model when anything changes, and gives each release one word: PROMOTE, REVIEW or BLOCK.

And remember who’s behind that long catalogue. The clouds aren’t a wall in front of your chip. They could be its customers. We think most of them would gladly run on more than one kind of chip, if the models ran correctly on it.

So the next time someone asks “Will it run Llama?”, you’ll have a better answer than yes or no. Which family it belongs to. What it would take. How you’ll know it’s right. One family at a time. That’s how we’re doing it too.
